# Register and redundancy

## What this family governs

Three faults sit together here because they share one reader. The first is hype and self-praise, where an adjective such as *novel*, *world-leading* or *remarkable* stands in for the number or comparison that would have earned it. The second is empty filler, doubled synonyms, category nouns and announcing frames, which cost a word of attention and return nothing. The third is the same point restated across abstract, introduction and conclusion with the wording drifting each time, so the reader cannot tell three claims from one. The family also carries the discipline that keeps these fixes from doing harm, because the deletion rules here are the ones most likely to change a claim while looking like tidying.

## The mechanism

Redundancy is a cost paid in attention and in claim-counting. A reader processes every word fully before discovering it carried nothing, so filler is not free even when it is invisible to the author who wrote it. The sharper cost arrives when a claim is restated in drifted wording. Meeting a familiar-looking sentence, the reader has to retrieve the original and decide whether the second version is identical or subtly different, and either answer forces a re-read. Word-for-word repetition makes that decision instant. Near-repetition with unexplained variation is the expensive case, and at document scale it inflates one finding into an apparent three. From this you can derive most of the sentence-level rules yourself — anything whose deletion leaves the propositions unchanged was carrying attention cost only, and anything restated needs either deletion or an explicit marker plus the original wording.

Register is a cost paid in credibility, and it runs through the reader's inference rather than their attention. Readers apply their own judgement to results and resist having it supplied, so a superlative about your own work sends them looking for the reason. Not finding one, they discount the claim. Worse, the evaluative adjective occupies the slot where a checkable fact would have sat, and the substitution reads as the fact being unavailable. The grant evidence makes this concrete — NIH bars its own reviewers from writing laudatory comments about investigators, so the praise you plant cannot enter the record even if believed. The same inference explains the emotive-word rule, the complaint rule and the dismissive-criticism rule. Each supplies the reader's reaction, or supplies a judgement about people where a property of a study design belongs.

## A hard boundary this family must respect

Filler-deletion rules are the most dangerous class in the ledger. Williams' concision list mixes genuinely empty tics with hedges and near-quantifiers — *virtually*, *generally*, *practically*, *certain*, *almost* — and deleting those does not tidy a sentence. It strengthens the claim. "Virtually all" is a real hedge distinct from "all", and the edit that removes it pushes an assertion past its evidence while looking like a word count fix. The negation rule fails the same way, because litotes and statistical negatives have no affirmative equivalent and "not significant" must never become "small". Four rules on this page carry that warning in the ledger itself.

State the boundary plainly. Any deletion that touches a hedge, a tentative modal, or an intensifier sitting on a graded absolute is query-only and never automatic. The pass reports the site and asks the author. It does not edit.

## The rules, grouped by mechanism

### Register — 11 rules

| Hype adjective (novel, critical, key, scalable) does the work of a quantity or comparison | Keep the adjective, add a number, named comparator or consequence in the same sentence | Density itself associates with funding (Qiu), so add information rather than delete | **Empirical.** Millar 2022 JAMA Netw Open — 130 of 139 hype adjectives rose across 901,717 abstracts of *successful NIH applications* 1985-2020, +7,690 words per million aggregate; Vinkers 2015 BMJ replicates with flat negative controls |
| Applicant mirrors reviewer praise vocabulary (standout, agentic, ability words) to earn a score | Drop the mirroring, spend the space on promotional density and on closing unaddressed weaknesses | — | **Empirical.** Kaatz 2015 Acad Med — 454 coded R01 critiques at *one institution*; funded applications drew significantly more ability, agentic, standout and positive-evaluation words, and fewer negative ones. The words are the reviewer's, written after the judgement |
| Self-praise about your own team (outstanding, world-leading, pioneering, internationally recognised) | Replace each adjective with the verifiable fact — the result, the number, the named award and what it recognises | A named honour is a fact; ERC asks applicants to explain an award's importance | NIH Simplified Review Framework reviewer guide 2026; ERC IfA V11.0 §2.2 |
| Emotive words (exciting, dramatic, groundbreaking) in the technical part | Replace with the property that produced the feeling; keep at most a couple in the significance part | The significance part is subjective by design | Abbott, Adelaide technical writing guide; Temple, Applying to NSF |

| Prose registers as promotional or as an engineering report | State what was built and what was measured in plain declaratives | — | reviewers, ICLR 2024/2025 (2 occurrences). | Superlatives about the paper's own contributions (novel, powerful, elegant, the first to) | Delete the adjective and state the property that justifies it | A checkable priority claim is a fact, and hedging it loses information | Knuth *Mathematical Writing* point 12; Williams L9 Intensifiers. **Flagged unsafe** — deletes unconditionally, discarding accurate wording where a number does support the claim |
| First person or past accomplishments in an NIH Project Summary | Recast in third person, move prior work to the Research Strategy | Several funders require a first-person plain-language summary | NIH Application Guide FORMS-I §7. **Flagged unsafe and funder-conditional** — applied to a UKRI or Wellcome summary it creates the violation |
| "I" where the author's persona is not the subject, or a passive built only to dodge a pronoun | Use "we" for the authors' action, reserve the passive for genuine agent-irrelevance or cohesive order | The passive is right when the agent is unknown, irrelevant or already established | Knuth point 6; Williams L4-L5. **Flagged unsafe** — core scope hits single-author grant text where the funder requires "I" |

### Redundancy at word and sentence scale — 10 rules

| Test | Fix | Counter-case | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Doubled pairs (full and complete, each and every, any and all) | Keep the member whose register matches the surrounding prose | "Complete and correct" names two separable properties in a specification | Williams L9 principle 2 |
| Modifier already inside the word (final outcome, past history, various different), or a category noun after an adjective (pink in colour, period of time) | Delete the modifier; convert adjective plus category into an adverb or noun | A live contrast makes the modifier work — "past performance" against "projected performance" | Williams L9 principles 3-4 |
| Sentence announces what the paper will do rather than asserting something | Strip the frame and promote the remainder to a claim; if the remainder is self-evident the paragraph needs a claim | Signposting is necessary at section openings and in a contributions list | Williams L9 Quick Tip on metadiscourse |

### Redundancy at section and document scale — 11 rules

| Test | Fix | Counter-case | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| A statement is restated later in slightly different words with no marker saying it is the same | Delete it, or mark it as a restatement and repeat the original wording exactly | Word-for-word repetition is a virtue for contrast, provided the difference is named | Halmos §12, "Do and do not repeat" |
| Same point, paragraph or formula restated, or prose verbose enough that a reviewer proposes condensing | Delete every sentence adding no new proposition, reinvest the space in method detail reviewers ask for | Repetition that saves a page jump, such as restating the loss where the experiments use it | reviewers, ICLR 2024/2025 (7 occurrences) |
| Familiar background explained at length while novel content is compressed or deferred | Budget space by novelty — one sentence for standard background | Cross-community submissions, where assigned reviewers cannot look the background up | reviewers, ICLR 2024/2025 (3 occurrences) |

### Restraint — 7 rules that govern the other 32

| Test | Fix | Counter-case | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Filler set — actually, basically, really, virtually, particular, certain, various, given, generally, practically, quite. Delete each and check whether meaning changed | Delete only the occurrences where nothing changed | "Virtually all" is a genuine hedge distinct from "all" | Williams L9 principle 1. **Flagged unsafe** — the list mixes empty filler with hedges and near-quantifiers |
| Negated constructions, especially two negations in one clause | Rewrite as the affirmative that means the same | Keep the negative where the point is the denial, or where the affirmative overstates. "Not significant" must not become "small" | Williams L9 principle 5. **Flagged unsafe** — litotes and statistical negatives have no affirmative equivalent |
| For any edit that is not an outright error correction, ask what would break on revert | Revert every change that only substitutes a preference | Voice yields where it obstructs purpose, such as a hedge making a safety-critical instruction ambiguous | PES 2016 C5/C11, IPEd D2 — marked superseded, re-verify before citing |
| A word is odd in context but has a reading on which the author meant it | Query the word, do not replace it | An obvious keyboard slip is a single-answer fix | Editors Canada, Ethical Editing of Graduate Student Texts, rev. 2024 |
| A word is unusual but correct | Query only if you can state why the plainer word is better, and put that reason in the query | Where the word is also wrong in sense, the diction rule applies | Chicago Manual Q&A, five-dollar words |
| Decide whether a pattern is a style choice or a readability failure | Intervene on readability even where the pattern is deliberate; leave style choices alone | On assessed student work, flag rather than rewrite | Harnby, repetition and authorial style |
| The proposed change cuts redundancy, flips a passive, or trims wordiness | Do none of these unless the substantive level was agreed; raise them as notes | Justified at any level once wordiness has become a readability failure | World Bank Editorial Style Guide 2020 App. A.1, Level B licences |

## What the corpus and reviewers add

The corpus is thin here, and it should be read as evidence of frequency rather than as worked examples. Nine of the 39 rules come from real revisions, and most of the recorded pairs are fragments a few words long — `is known to gain` becoming `gains`, `the frontier itself` becoming `it`, `\colorbox{green!15}{Redundancy,` becoming `Redundancy,`. One carries a real measurement. The compression shape behind the trailing-clause rule is the single most frequent edit in the whole corpus at 215 occurrences, and it dominates in the length-capped proposal, where `bear on sharing, each with its own measure.` became `touch sharing from different angles.` Two rows are not edits at all but supervisor comments left in the source. On the apologetic limitations passage the note reads `this reads apologetic and a minus point for our work. it's important to describe the limitations but it should be done in a more subtle way`, and on the replication dump the exchange runs from `all of the technical training details (besides the data) should probably be in the appendix` to the reply that most of it matters for replication but is `too boring to have space and unnecessary to understand`. Those two mark real supervisor-level faults with no repaired text to copy.

The reviewer evidence is frequency without examples. Restatement and verbosity drew 7 complaints across ICLR 2024 and 2025, over-explained background drew 3, and promotional or engineering-report register drew 2. No before/after pair survives for any of them. The register complaint is the weakest of the three and is marked not actionable in the ledger, because a verdict that framing feels showy names no sentence to change.

## How to run the pass by hand

Run the mechanical passes first, because they are cheap and their results do not depend on the argument.

1. Sweep the fixed lists — doubled pairs, redundant modifiers, category nouns, filler. Delete only what changes no meaning, and route every hedge and near-quantifier to a query list instead of deleting it.
2. Strip announcing frames and read what remains. A paragraph whose remainder is not a claim needs thinking rather than editing.
3. Count acronyms and delete any used fewer than three times.
4. Strip draft scaffolding in its own pass.
5. Run the cross-section restatement check. Extract every claim sentence from the abstract, the introduction's contribution list, and the conclusion, and lay the three sets side by side. For each abstract claim, find its counterparts. Identical wording is fine. Drifted wording is the fault, and the repair is to pick one phrasing and use it verbatim in all three places, or to delete two of the three. Where the drift turns out to encode a real difference in scope, that difference was never stated and now must be.
6. Read the register pass separately and only for your own work. Every evaluative adjective about your team, your method or your results gets one question — the fact that would justify it. Put the fact in and take the adjective out.
7. Scan for the three grant-specific register faults, which are complaints about circumstances, dismissive criticism of named prior work, and emotive words inside a technical section.
8. Finally, list every background paragraph no later sentence refers back to. Report the list and its length. Do not cut it.

## Where this family is weak

Most of the grant rules rest on one document each. The complaint rule comes from Kraicer alone, the acronym rule from Wellcome alone, the CV-shaping rule from a single UKRI opportunity page, the emotive-word split from Abbott's guide with Temple as loose support. The minimal-change rule cites a superseded 2016 standard with no verified 2024 equivalent, so it needs re-checking before it is quoted to anyone. Six rules are funder-conditional and one of those, the NIH third-person abstract rule, actively creates a violation if applied to a Wellcome or UKRI plain-language summary. Four more are flagged unsafe in the ledger itself.

The evidence linking register to outcomes has a harder limit. Millar measured hype only in *successful* NIH abstracts, so the rise is real but there is no unfunded comparison group and nothing in that study says hype helped. Kaatz measured reviewers' words rather than applicants', at one institution, and the word-to-score correlations reached significance only in the experienced-female-PI subsample with every male-subsample correlation null. Qiu's density association points the opposite way from the deletion advice. So the register mechanism as stated — a reviewer reads self-praise as a substitute for evidence — is well supported by what funders instruct their reviewers to do and poorly supported by measured outcomes. Treat it as a compliance argument and a plausible inference. Do not treat it as a demonstrated effect on scores.
