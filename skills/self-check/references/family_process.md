# The reviewer's procedure

## What these rules govern

These 28 rules say almost nothing about what good prose looks like. They govern the reviewer's own conduct — which findings may be repaired in silence, which have to go back to the author as a question, and how that question must be written to be answerable at all. The distinction carries more weight for a supervisor than for a hired copyeditor, because the supervisor holds power over the author that the copyeditor does not. A student reads a supervisor's silent rewrite as an instruction and accepts it whether or not it is right, and on assessed work the credit for the writing travels with the rewrite. Editors Canada maintains a separate and deliberately stricter default for graduate student text for exactly that reason, and it is the only rule in this family where authorship integrity rather than the text does the deciding.

## The governing test

Count the admissible repairs for the problem. Exactly one possible correction makes it a fixing case, and the reviewer edits without asking. Two or more defensible solutions make it a flagging case, and the reviewer hands the decision back with the span quoted. SENSE states this as the boundary of the whole profession — direct fixing applies where only one correct answer is possible, and flagging applies where more than one solution is possible. Harnby arrives at the same line from the practitioner side, asking whether you are unsure how to repair the problem and whether you think several repairs exist, and answering that the query trumps the amendment every time either condition holds.

The reason cardinality is the right test rather than a proxy for one is that it is observable from the text itself. Judging it never requires guessing what the author intended. It also lines the two failure modes up cleanly. A single-answer fix cannot misrepresent the author, since there was no choice available to misrepresent. A multi-answer fix always can, and worse, it leaves no record that a choice was made.

Harnby's payoff comparison shows why the boundary is not symmetric around the midpoint. Work out what happens under all four combinations — query or change, right or wrong. Querying looks diligent whichever way the uncertainty resolves. Changing looks harmful the moment you turn out to be wrong. Since a query costs a line and a wrong change costs the text and the relationship, the risk-weighted choice tips toward the query well before the reviewer is actually in doubt.

Most of the 28 rules follow from that test plus three refinements. First, the count is not fixed by nature. A house style sheet, an agreed brief, or an explicit convention converts a named multi-answer item into a single-answer one, and then it is fixed silently like any typo. This is why SENSE's split between correctness and formulation works — correctness breaks answer to "we do not say this in English" and have one answer, while formulation breaks answer to "we do not say this in academia" and are checkable only against a target community the author knows better than you do. Collapsing the two is how style preference travels under the authority of correctness. Second, cardinality decides permission and not legibility. A change can be safe on cardinality grounds and still be invisible in its reasoning, which is what SENSE's third category is for — fix and flag together, correcting the text and attaching a one-line reason so the author can accept it knowingly. Third, the reviewer's own limits are grounds for flagging even where the text has one answer. SENSE names three such triggers, namely that the text is unclear so only the author can decide, that the issue sits outside the agreed remit, or that the issue recurs across the text. Each names a limit on the reviewer rather than a defect in the writing. Recurrence is the trigger most often mishandled, because the individually easy fix conceals a convention the author should own.

Two boundaries keep the test from running away. Uncertainty about a mechanical rule is resolved by looking the rule up, and never by querying the author. And a habit of querying everything shifts the entire cost onto the reader of the query list, which Editors Canada treats as its own failure.

## An empirical corroboration worth stating

A separate analysis of 200 real cross-author edits, sampled from 19,315 revisions by 382 machine-learning researchers, found that roughly 19 percent of all edits are not edits. They are a note left at the exact site of a problem the editor could not resolve, addressed to whoever owns the answer. Four independent samples of 50 found the pattern at 6+4, 8, 4 and 10 instances. Nobody in that corpus had read the SENSE guidelines or any of the other standards here. The professional standard and observed expert practice were derived independently and agree, which is the strongest warrant either one has. Note also that none of the 28 rules in this family carries a frequency figure of its own — the corroboration comes entirely from that outside sample.

### Fix or flag — the cardinality boundary (6 rules)

| Test | Action | Counter-case | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Count admissible repairs. One means fix, two or more means flag | Apply cardinality as the first gate on every finding | A multi-answer problem recurring on nearly every page is settled once by a batched note naming the convention adopted | SENSE p.4 |
| The finding matches one of three triggers — unclear text, out of remit, or recurrent | Flag, and state in the note which of the three applies | A recurrent problem with one admissible answer, such as a consistently misspelled term, is fixed throughout and reported once | SENSE p.2 |
| Single-answer fix whose reason a reader would not immediately see | Fix and flag together, correction plus a one-line reason | Obvious typo repairs need no note, and annotating them buries the notes that matter | SENSE p.2 |
| Ask which norm breaks — correctness (English) or formulation (academia) | Fix correctness silently, route formulation to the author | A style sheet or explicit brief converts named formulation items into single-answer fixes | SENSE p.4 |
| Compare the four outcomes of query-or-change against right-or-wrong | Default to the query wherever a wrong change would harm | PES A12.2 caps this, since querying everything is its own failure | Harnby, superhero |

### What belongs to the author alone (3 rules)

| Test | Action | Counter-case | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Graduate student text and the finding is mechanics — comma splice, agreement, sentence combining | Err toward flagging, leave the revision to the student | Non-assessed writing inverts this default, and there mechanics are silent fixes | Editors Canada, Ethical Editing Part 1 p.2 |

### Writing a query that cannot be dismissed (7 rules)

| Test | Action | Counter-case | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Read the draft as the reviewer will — clear, tactful, succinct, actionable | Rewrite until it names the span, states the issue in a line, and asks for a specific action | Student-text guidance forbids drafting alternatives, so the offered solution is withheld on assessed work | Editors Canada PES A12.3 |
| Check for three parts — exact span, brief concern, specific question | Highlight the span, state the concern, name the action wanted | A manuscript-wide query has no single span, so the anchor becomes a described pattern with two or three located instances | ACES, Brenner 2019 |
| Would a one-word brush-off dispose of it | Identify the point of confusion precisely enough to remove the escape route | A genuine audience-level question is asked broadly, supplying the two readings you fear | ACES, Montgomerie 2019 |
| Scan for a pronoun standing in for the text — this, that, it | Quote the exact text inside the query | In a comment anchored to a highlighted span the pronoun is unambiguous, though quoting survives the move into a list | ACES, Ozunal 2020 |
| Can the query be understood where it sits, or does it send the reader elsewhere | Make each query self-contained, restating rather than pointing | A genuinely global decision is stated once in a cover note, and individual queries refer to it | ACES, Ozunal 2020 |
| Count solutions offered. Zero leaves a problem, more than one is a menu | State the issue briefly and provide one concrete solution | Editors Canada forbids drafted alternatives on assessed student work, and there the query offers none | ACES, Ozunal 2020 |
| The word is unfamiliar to you | Look it up first, act only if the dictionary does not support the usage | A term that is correct but opaque to the readership still earns an accessibility query after the lookup | Littleford, 7 golden rules |

| Test | Action | Counter-case | Source |
|---|---|---|---|

## The tension this family contains, and how it resolves

Two of the sources here pull against each other, and both pulls are real. Editors Canada tells editors of graduate student work to err heavily toward flagging rather than fixing, on the ground that the text is evidence of the student's own ability — the same comma splice that is a silent fix in a trade book is a query in a thesis. Harnby pushes the other way, holding that leaving text alone because it is the author's style cannot be justified, and that readability outranks deference. That counterweight is what licenses intervention where an inherited choice is a readability failure rather than a preference.

The precedence runs authorship risk first, then readability, then deference. Authorship risk wins outright, which in practice means that on any text being assessed, mechanics stay with the student even when you know the single correct repair. That is the one place in this family where cardinality is overruled. Readability comes second, and it is what stops deference from becoming an excuse — a passage a reader cannot follow is a defect regardless of whose style produced it, and the reviewer either repairs it or raises a query that names the specific span. Deference comes last and is a tiebreak only. It applies where the text works, the repair is multi-answer, and nothing but taste separates the options. Deference is never a reason to stay silent about something the reader will trip over.

## How to run a review by hand

1. Fix the remit before reading. Note the stage, the scope, and whether the text is assessed. Assessed text puts mechanics out of your hands.
2. Read once for global questions. Anything affecting the whole text or a large part of it goes to the author immediately, because it is a precondition for the rest of your work.
3. Work the text, and for each finding count the admissible repairs. One means fix. Two or more means flag.
4. Before raising any factual or reference query, spend five minutes trying to answer it yourself. Look up every unfamiliar word before you either change or query it. Query only what survives.
5. Run the validity gate on each surviving candidate. Name the consequence of leaving the problem alone. Where you cannot name one, drop the query.
6. Draft each query with the three parts, namely the quoted span, the concern in one line, and a specific question naming the action wanted. Offer exactly one concrete solution unless the text is assessed.
7. Check every query for a pronoun standing in for the text. Replace this, that and it with the quoted words. Then read the query as the author will and ask whether a one-word brush-off would dispose of it.
8. Check that each query can be understood where it sits. Restate the point instead of sending the author to another page.
9. Sort what remains. Major queries go out now. Minor ones go in one batch, weighed for volume so the important entries do not get skimmed past.
10. Attach a one-line reason to any silent fix whose grounds a reader would not immediately see.

## Where this family is weak

Every one of these 28 rules was written for a two-party arrangement, namely one editor and one author, inside a publishing pipeline with named stages and a production editor to appeal to. The reader of this page is usually in a different situation altogether — several co-authors editing one shared file at the same time, with no stage boundaries, no signed-off earlier version, and no one whose job it is to arbitrate. The standards say nothing about that. Rule A1.3 assumes an ordered chain where each editor's turn is separate and identifiable, so it has no advice for two people revising the same paragraph within the hour. Stage discipline under A2.5 and E1.7 presumes stages exist to be violated. The selective-tracking rule from Wesleyan presumes a tracked-changes tool with one author to approve the marks. Batching under CIEP 5.3.2(a) presumes one recipient with a schedule.

There are two further gaps worth naming. The family carries no frequency data of its own, so the relative weight of these rules in real practice rests on the outside sample described above rather than on anything internal. And all 28 are marked decidable with no confidence tier assigned to any of them, which means the file offers no ranking to fall back on where two rules point opposite ways. The precedence stated in the tension section above was reasoned from the sources rather than read off the data.
