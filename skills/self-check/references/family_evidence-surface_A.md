# Evidence surface — first half

Rules are numbered by their line in `fam_evsurf_a.jsonl`, 1 to 49. Provenance in this half runs 24 editing-authorities, 11 corpus, 10 grant-funders, 3 empirical, 1 local-extension.

## 1. What this family governs

This family governs the surface where evidence meets the reader — figures, tables, captions, axes, error terms, reported numbers, and the citations that attach a claim to something checkable. It is the part of a paper or proposal a reader reaches first and often the only part a skimming reader reaches at all. A failure here leaves the reader either unable to recover the number the author meant (a 3 pt axis label, a legend that was never defined, a plus-minus term of unstated meaning) or recovering a different number with confidence (a truncated bar axis, a 3D column, a mean over a bimodal latency distribution). The second failure is the expensive one, because nothing on the page tells the reader that their reading is wrong. A grant version of the same failure hands the reviewer an unattributed figure or an unquantified activity, and the feasibility judgement then rests on nothing the reviewer can inspect.

## 2. The mechanism

A number or a graph is a claim in compressed form. The author starts with a full object — a set of runs, each with its own seed, input size, checkpoint and measured value — and ships a small artefact standing in for it. Compression is lossy by construction, and the reader reconstructs the missing part using defaults rather than using the author's intent. Every rule in this family is a repair to one of a small number of places where the default reconstruction diverges from what was measured.

Four things get thrown away, and naming them is enough to derive most of the family yourself.

The first is the population. A mean, a plus-minus term, an interval or a correlation coefficient is a statement about a set of observations, and the artefact carries the statement while dropping the set. The reader has no way to tell a standard deviation from a range, five training seeds from five evaluation passes over one checkpoint, or three points from three hundred. Since the interval's whole job is to say how much the number would move if the study were redone, an interval computed over things that were not independently redone is narrower than the claim it is offered for. From that single observation you can derive the rules about naming what the plus-minus measures, about reporting n, about replicates versus runs, about plotting the individual points when n is three, and about pairing — because when the same seeds appear in both conditions, the between-seed spread is shared and cancels in the difference, so per-condition bars are wider than the contrast warrants.

The second is the mapping from quantity to visual channel. A figure encodes each number in a channel, and the channels are not equally readable. Cleveland and McGill measured the order — position on a common scale, then position on a non-aligned scale, then length, direction and angle, then area, then volume and curvature, then shading and colour saturation. The channel therefore sets a ceiling on how accurately the number can be recovered, which makes channel choice a claim about how precisely the reader is invited to read. Anything that changes the geometry without changing the data changes the recovered value. Depth on a bar swaps length for rendered volume, so apparent size grows faster than height. A truncated baseline multiplies apparent difference by a factor the author picked. A log axis converts multiplication into addition, which is right for growth order and destroys a constant factor. Each of those is derivable from the same sentence, and so is the ink budget — every non-data mark occupies a channel the eye must first reject.

The third is what the marks assert beyond the data. A drawn element carries a proposition, and the proposition is often stronger than the experiment. A line between two points asserts that the space between them is populated and that the axes are related by a function, which fails outright for unordered categories. A bar asserts that length is proportional to value, which is what forces a zero origin. A shared shape across two panels asserts a shared scale, so a reader who has learned one panel's geometry applies it to the next and a range change silently rescales the effect. A box shape reused for two kinds of thing in one diagram asserts that they are the same kind of thing.

The fourth is everything absent. Omission leaves no trace on the page. A window cropped where the trend suits the claim is locally accurate and globally false, and the reader has no signal that a wider window exists. This is why Tufte's diagnostic is a question about suspicion rather than a measurement, and it is the one part of the pass you cannot run from the rendered artefact alone. You have to run it against the logs.

A fifth, weaker fact governs the caption rules. Readers enter through the figures and read out of order, so a caption depending on the body text has no reliable antecedent at the moment it is read. Anything defined only in the method section is, for that reader, undefined.

## 3. A boundary the reader must know

Precision and rounding rules exist for one reason. Reporting more digits than the measurement error supports tells the reader the quantity is known more finely than it is. That reasoning applies only to measured quantities. An exact value has no measurement error, so it has no excess precision to trim. A rounding or precision-harmonising rule that reaches a count, a random seed, a parameter or byte size, a hash, an identifier, a version number, or an epoch or step index destroys information rather than tidying it, and a rounded seed cannot be recovered. Classify every number as measured or exact before applying any precision rule, and skip the exact ones (rule 38, local-extension). One concession — a very large exact count may be given approximately in running prose, provided the exact value appears in a table or the appendix. The warrant for the boundary is internal. Two rounding rules elsewhere in this ledger, one from Zobel and one from the statistics-reporting standards, were independently flagged unsafe for exactly this, and one of them named counts, seeds and byte sizes explicitly.

## 5. What the corpus adds

Eleven rules here came from real revisions of this researcher's own papers rather than from a guide, and they add two things a guide cannot. The first is frequency evidence. Rule 39 records the same reviewer asking for the fitted values of `$\alpha$`, `$\beta_A$` and `$\beta_B$` in four separate commits before they were supplied, which is direct evidence that normal rereading does not catch the gap. Rule 43 records three separate repositories shipping an empty cite command through many commits, so the bare bracket is not one author's slip. Rule 46 records two reviewers raising appendix imbalance independently, one of them twice.

The second is that the resolutions are visible, so the counter-cases are grounded rather than imagined. Four pairs are worth reading directly.

Rule 40 moved from `\[name]{We should define what the $\pm$ values represent at first use (e.g., max spread, standard deviation, range across strategies).}` to shipped text reading `deviations of only $\pm0.7$ percentage points (pp) of probing accuracy`.

Rule 43 moved from a literal `\citep{}.` to `\citep{rippel2014nested,kusupati2022matryoshka,godey2026matryoshka}.`

Rule 45 moved from `WildChat,` to `WildChat \citep{zhao2024wildchat},` — a supervisor-raised note, and the smallest possible fix.

Rule 49 is the most instructive, because the note states the principle and the resolution follows its shape exactly. The note reads `\[name]{in all captions, we can state a bottom line shortly at the beginning, but the main body should describe the figure not the results}`. The resulting caption opens `Merging token spaces via WWT yields substantial gains in cross-lingual knowledge generalization. Top: \ceq for English--Arabic pretraining under each method (colors), measuring how much an Arabic exposure to a fact is worth for recalling it in English.` One bottom line, then a panel-by-panel description.

Rule 47's note also shows a reviewer catching two defects at once — `\[name]{there's win rate several times on y axis (sub and main) and the rows all look the same. Also why JPEG and not PDF? Doesn't render well}`. Rule 39's resolution was an explicit deferral, `\[name]{add the fitted values for a representative experiment in the appendix}`, which is how these items usually close.

## 6. How to run the pass by hand

Work from the artefacts inward, in five sweeps, and do the sweeps in this order because each one narrows what the next has to look at.

Start with the classification the boundary in section 3 requires. Walk every number in the draft and mark it measured or exact, because a precision decision made before that classification is a decision made wrong. Second, render every figure at its final printed width and check the mechanical properties — smallest text, line weight, vector format, caption placement, caption length, and legibility in greyscale. These are properties of the file rather than of the argument, so they are the cheapest to fix and the easiest to batch. Third, cover the body text and read each figure and table with its caption alone, writing down the one sentence it is meant to make you believe. Anything you cannot decode without the body text is a caption defect, and anything whose sentence you cannot write is a figure that has no message. Fourth, for every reported spread, mean, interval and correlation, name what was varied and count it. A spread over evaluation passes of one checkpoint, a mean over mixed input sizes, and per-condition bars in a seed-matched design are the three that recur. Fifth, and only this sweep needs the logs, ask what was measured and not shown — the wider window, the later steps, the condition omitted. Finish with the citation grep for empty brace pairs, which takes seconds and catches a defect that ships.

For a proposal, replace the third and fifth sweeps. Read the preliminary and prior-work sections asking whose data each item is, then asking which named limitation each carries, then asking whether each activity has a number and a named person attached.

## 7. Where this half is weak

Several rules rest on a single source with no corroboration, and they should be treated as good practice rather than as established. Rule 5 on interpolation, rule 6 on homogeneous table columns, rule 7 on tables never carrying essential information and rule 11 on means all rest on Zobel alone, and rule 7 is unusually strong for a single-source claim, since it forbids the results table most machine-learning venues expect. Rule 21 rests on Tufte alone. Rule 19 on plotting individual points at n = 3 rests on one rule of Cumming, Fidler and Vaux, whose own justification is that small samples are merely common. On the grant side, rule 35 is the weakest link in the half — the row states plainly that no funder in the sources reviewed prohibits "data not shown" by name, so the rule rests entirely on Kraicer's positive instruction to show the data. Rules 36 and 37 each rest on one writing guide, and rules 29, 32, 33, 34 and 36 are marked funder-conditional, so they bind only where that funder's rules apply.

Three places need a house-style decision rather than a fact. Rule 15's caption placement is set by the venue's style file, and some venues invert the convention outright, so the rule can only enforce consistency with whatever the template says. Rule 24's numeric floors conflict between its own two sources — Nature permits line weights from 0.25 pt while Science sets a 0.5 pt minimum, so a 0.3 pt rule passes one and fails the other, and the correct output depends on the target venue. The sharpest conflict is between rule 8 and rule 37. Rule 8 requires the caption to define every line style, symbol, colour, abbreviation and plus-minus term, and rule 23 adds n, the test name and the p value to that load, while rule 37 caps captions at about 60 words. A multi-panel figure cannot satisfy both, and rule 49's counter-case notes that some journals specify the caption as a findings statement, which is a third incompatible target. Rules 1 and 22 also pull against rule 30, since the first two remove gridlines the third's look-up reading would want.

Two of the three empirical rules carry their own contradictions, stated in their rows. Rule 27's effect is measured on award amount among already funded proposals, so it cannot speak to being funded at all, and its fewer-common-words direction runs against the jargon literature. Rule 26's Charles and Whiteside finding is contradicted by Connor on whether the value moves are obligatory in EU proposals. Rule 13's Cleveland and McGill ranking is partly overturned in its lower tiers by Skau and Kosara. Six rules in this half are flagged as needing a style sheet — 6, 8, 12, 15, 22 and 24 — which is the practical measure of how much of this family is convention rather than fact.
