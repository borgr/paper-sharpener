# Recommendation Letter Guide

## Contents

The tips for letters that recommend or evaluate a person. Sentence- and word-level craft is
not repeated here; it lives in `writing-guide.md` (the shared paper guide), and an entry below
names the guide entry it leans on. Each entry has the same parts: the **defect**, the **cue**
that finds it, the **repair**, the **boundary** where the rule stops, and **why**, with source
keys that resolve in `sources.md`.

Entries are ordered by level, which is the order of importance to the reader. Criticize from
the top: a fix at a higher level rewrites everything under it. Mechanics come last and are
fixed without asking.

### The model underneath

A letter is read twice. The first pass takes about a minute and decides whether the second
happens; the second is close and adversarial, pen in hand, noting what was said and what was
not [krishnamurthi; guo; timed-letters]. Praise is inflated almost everywhere — 97.5% of
1,224 geoscience postdoc letters were good or excellent [dutt2016] — so praise alone carries
almost nothing. Three things carry the signal, because each is costly to fake:

1. **A calibrated verdict.** A rare, explicit "top" placement predicts outcomes; in econ PhD
   job letters only 12% gave one, and it went with a 17-point higher chance of a top-20 job
   on a 6% base [hirtle-kovner].
2. **Evidence only the writer has.** Specific examples raise both the candidate's rating and
   the writer's credibility [knouse1983].
3. **The writer's own calibration.** Writer habits explain much of the variation between
   letters, and readers correct for them [hirtle-kovner; krishnamurthi].

Readers average claims rather than add them [weaver2012; obermaier-koch], so a weak claim
added to strong ones lowers the whole letter.

## 0. Genre and call

### Know which genre you are writing

**Defect.** The letter takes the stance of the wrong genre: advocacy in a tenure review,
praise in an immigration letter that needs criteria, a recommendation where a sponsor
commitment was asked for.

**Cue.** Name the genre before reading a word of the draft. `letter-types.md` lists them and
what each reverses.

**Repair.** Recommendation (admission, fellowship, postdoc, faculty job) advocates and ranks.
Evaluation (tenure, promotion, external review) assesses against the requesting
institution's standard, at arm's length, answering every question asked. Sponsor or host
letters (Azrieli, MSCA) commit resources and a mentoring plan. Visa expert letters map
evidence to the regulatory criteria. Award endorsements attest to contribution.

**Boundary.** Most letters are recommendations; ask only when the request does not say.

**Why.** Four genres reverse defaults that hold for the rest; a tenure letter hedged like a
recommendation reads as negative, and an advocacy letter in a tenure file reads as
conflicted (`letter-types.md`).

### Obey the call before anything else

**Defect.** The letter breaks a hard rule of the call: length cap, addressee, letterhead,
signature, language, a question the referee form asks and the letter does not answer.

**Cue.** Ask for the current call text. Check cap, addressee and every referee question.

**Repair.** Cut to the cap (Rhodes reads two pages, Marshall 1,000 words); address the named
body (Clore requires the Clore Scholars Programme); answer each question the form asks
(Rothschild: comparison with peers, your own students and past Fellows).

**Boundary.** Rules change every cycle; trust the call over `letter-types.md`.

**Why.** Over-length pages go unread; a missing answer reads as an answer (`letter-types.md`).

## 1. Verdict and calibration

### The first paragraph carries the verdict

**Defect.** The first paragraph spends itself on how the writer met the candidate, on
boilerplate, or on hedges; the strength of the endorsement arrives late or never.

**Cue.** Read the first paragraph alone. Can a one-minute reader say (a) who the writer is to
the candidate, how long and how closely, (b) how strong the endorsement is, (c) the single
most specific reason? Is anything in it negative?

**Repair.** Relationship in one clause, then the verdict with its calibrated rank if one
exists, then the sharpest piece of evidence. Nothing negative in the first paragraph. The
close reader gets the full case below.

**Boundary.** In evaluative genres the verdict is an assessment, stated as plainly, but not
as advocacy.

**Why.** First-pass reading is about a minute per letter [krishnamurthi; guo;
timed-letters]. A mild negative persuades only when it comes after the positives
[ein-gar2012]. Shared guide: *Start by stating the point of the paragraph*.

### A rank is believed only when it is rationed and reproducible

**Defect.** The letter ranks without a pool ("top 1%"), hedges the rank ("one of the
better"), ranks against celebrities or the whole field, or gives the same top rank the writer
gave others this cycle. Or it has no rank at all for a candidate who deserves one.

**Cue.** Find the rank sentence. Does it name the pool, its size and a time window? Is it
hedged? Is it a rank the writer could reproduce from a list of names? Has this writer used it
recently? For job letters, does it say what tier the candidate fits?

**Repair.** "The strongest of the roughly 25 MSc students I have worked with since 2019."
Add the base rate when it helps ("I have said this of three students"). For jobs, the tier:
"I would hire her into a department like ours." If the candidate is not near the top, omit the
rank; never write a middling one.

**Boundary.** Do not ration below the truth: in an inflated field, a strict writer who gives
no rank hurts their own students. The base rate is what lets the reader correct for it.

**Why.** The rare explicit placement carries the outcome effect [hirtle-kovner].
Forced-choice ranks inflate until they stop discriminating — in emergency-medicine standardized
letters 58% of applicants sat in the top two of four bins [sloe-inflation; shappell2024] — as
cheap-talk theory predicts [kartik2007; chan2007]. Time-bounded, sized superlatives beat broad
cohort ranks [fortnow]; famous-name comparisons backfire [leiter-thread].

### Your credentials calibrate, they do not impress

**Defect.** The writer paragraph lists status (h-index, titles, collaborator counts) without
telling the reader how to decode the writer's scale. Or its facts are stale (an "incoming" title,
an old email, a count that no longer matches), or it sits as a footnote inside the verdict
sentence, where it interrupts the one sentence the reader must not lose.

**Cue.** Remove the writer paragraph: does the reader lose information about the candidate's
standing, or only about the writer's? Check its title, email and counts against the writer
profile. Check where its footnote mark falls.

**Repair.** Give the comparison pool, how often you write letters this strong, and where past
students went. Keep one status line; readers do weigh seniority. Update stale facts from the
profile. Move a footnote mark off the verdict sentence, to the writer's name or the end of the
first paragraph.

**Boundary.** Where the call asks for the referee's standing (tenure, visa), status is
evidence and belongs.

**Why.** Readers calibrate writers by habit and track record [hirtle-kovner; krishnamurthi;
nicklin-roch]; prestige alone transfers status rather than information.

### Swap the candidate, not the words

**Defect.** The verdict, rank, top descriptors or length would differ for an equally strong
candidate of another gender, ethnicity or nationality. Ability is described in effort words
only (hardworking, diligent) or in caring words only (helpful, kind). Personal life appears
where it does not bear on the role.

**Cue.** The swap test: rewrite the rank sentence and the three strongest descriptors for a
candidate of another group with the same record; would you write them the same? Check that
at least one ability or originality word ("creative", "original", "a scientist") is present
and earned. Scan for family, appearance, health, age.

**Repair.** Give the same rank and the same kind of words to the same record. Pair effort
with what it achieved. Remove personal details that do not bear on the role.

**Boundary.** Effort words are fine beside ability claims. Word-list ratios are advisory and
are never reported as "no bias". Personal context the candidate asked you to explain (a gap,
a hardship) belongs.

**Why.** The largest group gaps sit in the top verdict itself — women 18% less likely to get a
"top department" recommendation in econ letters [hirtle-kovner]; half as likely to get an
"excellent" letter in geoscience [dutt2016]. Word-list differences replicate unevenly and can
cancel within a list [bernstein2022; zhao2023]; letters for women carried more doubt raisers
[trix-psenka; madera2019].

## 2. Evidence

### A claim the reader acts on needs a story only you could tell

**Defect.** The letter asserts traits ("brilliant", "independent", "a compelling speaker")
without evidence, or tells a formulaic story that could describe anyone ("faced with a
challenging bug, she persisted and solved it").

**Cue.** List the claims a committee would act on: the rank and the two or three headline
traits. For each, is there a story or a calibrating number? Does each story hold a detail
only the writer could know — a date, a decision, a disagreement, what surprised them?

**Repair.** Two or three stories, each tied to a claim. Show the consequence in others'
behavior ("labmates asked him to train the group in giving talks"). Prefer the typical story
to the most dramatic one, and say it was typical. Numbers only when they calibrate: pool size,
rank, acceptance rate, adoption.

**Boundary.** Not every adjective needs a story; the claims the reader acts on do. Where
contact was thin, write a shorter letter with one sourced observation and say what it
covers; do not stretch thin contact into narrative.

**Why.** Specific examples raised ratings and writer credibility; numeric data alone did not
[knouse1983]. Vivid but non-diagnostic detail persuades beyond its information
[dana2013], hence typical over dramatic. Generic fluency reads as machine-written and is
marked down [preiksaitis2023]. Shared guide: *Examples are easier than abstractions*.

### Describe the research, and translate its currency

**Defect.** Papers are listed without the candidate's role; venues and status are blurred
(preprint, workshop, main track); conventions a non-CS committee cannot decode go
unexplained (conferences as archival venues, author order, equal contribution).

**Cue.** For the one or two key works: is the problem described in the writer's words? The
candidate's own part? Whether it worked? The venue, its selectivity and the exact status? The
author-order convention?

**Repair.** "The paper, accepted at [venue] (the field's main peer-reviewed venue, [rate]% acceptance
that year), is co-first-authored; she designed the evaluation and ran every experiment."
For software: who uses it, what it enabled, one number with its source.

**Boundary.** CS readers need less translation than university-wide or foundation
committees (Rothschild, Clore, Azrieli, tenure); translate for the reader the call names.

**Why.** Hiring rests on the top one or two papers and the candidate's role in them
[cra2015]; conferences are archival in CS [cra1999]; students blur venue status and readers
notice [krishnamurthi]; software needs impact stated [puebla2024].

### Say what the file cannot

**Defect.** The letter restates the CV or transcript ("did well in class", publication
lists, grades).

**Cue.** For each paragraph: is this in the CV or transcript already?

**Repair.** Replace with what only the writer saw: ownership, judgement, how the candidate
handled being wrong, growth over time, working style. Put grades in context only where the
context is the news (rank in a sized class, a hard course taken early).

**Boundary.** A short contextual clause about a record item is fine when it carries the
writer's judgement.

**Why.** "Did well in class" letters are near-worthless [krishnamurthi; manohar]; readers
want what the file does not show [rhodes; cambridge].

### The close reader looks for what is missing

**Defect.** The genre expects an item and the letter is silent on it: no rank, no research
role, no word on independence for a PhD or postdoc, no teaching for a faculty letter, no
answer to a referee-form question. Or the letter counts items it never names: "three
findings", of which one is described, one reappears later as "other projects", and one is
never mentioned.

**Cue.** Take the genre's expected items from `letter-types.md` and tick them off. For every
count in the letter, find each counted item and check that the reader can tell it is one of
them.

**Repair.** Add the item with evidence, or, if the writer cannot assess it, one line saying
so ("I have not seen her teach"). Name every counted item where the count appears, and refer
back to the count where an item is described later ("The third paper…").

**Boundary.** A deliberate silence the writer chose is their call; surface it, do not fill it.

**Why.** Second readers note what was not said [krishnamurthi]; omission reads as a hidden
negative [fortnow; orzel].

## 3. Selection

### Cut claims, not evidence

**Defect.** Mild positives follow strong evidence (punctuality, a B+ course, "pleasant to
work with"), or a weak paragraph covers a dimension the writer cannot really assess.

**Cue.** Rank the paragraphs by strength. Flag any that adds no evidence at least as strong as
the weakest one the letter needs.

**Repair.** Cut the weak claim; keep strong or moderately strong specific evidence. Replace a
weak paragraph on an unassessed dimension with one line saying it was not assessed.

**Boundary.** Moderately strong, specific items still help; only weak or non-diagnostic ones
dilute.

**Why.** Readers average rather than add [weaver2012; weaver2016]; adding weak arguments
lowered support while moderate ones raised it [obermaier-koch]; non-diagnostic detail
dilutes [nisbett1981]. Shared guide: *Kill your darlings, cut unless serving the story*.

### No hedges; at most one real limitation

**Defect.** Hedges about ability or potential ("might", "seems", "hopefully", "could become",
"I hope my guidance helped"), scattered negatives ("although she can be stubborn"), undercutting
asides ("grades are not everything"), or faint praise as the strongest descriptor
("competent", "solid", "can do the job").

**Cue.** Mark every hedge, negative or minimal-assurance phrase attached to the candidate.
`letter_check.py` finds the lexical ones.

**Repair.** State what you know directly; delete what you would not stand behind. At most one
limitation, real, mild and specific, placed late and framed as growth with evidence that it
is improving — and only for junior candidates. Never invent one.

**Boundary.** Removing a hedge strengthens a claim, and the claim belongs to the writer: ask,
never strip silently. Evaluative genres state real limitations plainly. Senior candidates
(faculty, tenure files) get no volunteered limitation unless asked.

**Why.** One hedging or negative sentence lowered rated research competence in an experiment
with 305 professors [madera2019]; letters for accepted students stated productivity with more
certainty [houser-lemmons]. Letters with examples plus one unfavorable statement were among
the best received [knouse1983], and a mild negative after positives can raise persuasion
[ein-gar2012]. Shared guide: *Use strengthening words sparingly to emphasize what you really
care about*.

### Tailor the evidence, not the rank

**Defect.** The letter was written for another call, swaps only the institution's name, or
moves the rank with the prestige of the target.

**Cue.** Does any paragraph map evidence to the call's stated criteria? Is anything
addressed to a different program? Is the rank the same as in the master letter?

**Repair.** Keep a master letter and rewrite one paragraph per call, choosing the evidence
that answers that call's criteria. The rank stays.

**Boundary.** Where the call asks for "rank within our program", answer that question and
keep it separate from your overall rank.

**Why.** Cosmetic tailoring is noticed and not valued [krishnamurthi]; program fit leaking
into ability ratings undermines them [sloe2-critique].

### Length follows evidence

**Defect.** The letter is padded toward an expected length, or cut so short it reads as
perfunctory.

**Cue.** Word count against the call's cap; every added paragraph must pass *Cut claims, not
evidence*.

**Repair.** Default ranges: 1.5–2 pages for admission, fellowship and postdoc letters, 2–3 for
faculty letters, 2–4 for tenure. A short honest letter about one thing says so.

**Boundary.** The call's cap overrides the default.

**Why.** Longer letters go with stronger evaluations and better outcomes [dutt2016;
akos-kretchmar], but length varies with writer culture and gender [zhao2023; bernstein2022],
and the gain comes from evidence, not words [obermaier-koch].

## 4. Voice

### Keep the letter yours

**Defect.** The draft holds claims the writer did not supply, reads as generic prose that
could be about anyone, or a revision has dropped or bent a point the writer was making.

**Cue.** Every factual claim traces to the writer's notes or the candidate's materials.
Every paragraph has a sentence that could not appear in another candidate's letter. For a
revised passage, list the original's points and tick each off in the new text.

**Repair.** Delete untraceable claims. Keep the writer's own phrasing and judgements; edit
for clarity, not for register. Where an aside reads as a hedge but carries a stance, restate
the stance positively rather than cut it.

**Boundary.** Light correction of non-native phrasing is fine; replacing the writer's voice
is not.

**Why.** Model-drafted letters add content that carries gender bias [wan2023]; writing with
language models homogenizes text [padmakumar-he]; readers rate letters they believe are human
higher [preiksaitis2023]; detectors misflag non-native English as machine text [liang2023].

### Explicit for American readers, not effusive

**Defect.** A reserved European or Israeli letter goes to American readers with no explicit
rank (read as faint praise), or an effusive letter goes to British readers (read as noise).

**Cue.** Who reads it? Is the rank explicit? How many superlatives are there?

**Repair.** For American readers, make the rank and the verdict explicit; do not raise the
temperature of the praise. For British and European readers, keep superlatives to the one
calibrated claim.

**Boundary.** No source documents Israeli conventions directly; this is inference from the
American and European evidence. When writer and committee are both Israeli, the reader knows
the writer's scale; keep the explicit rank and pool anyway, since Israeli calls increasingly
sit beside American-calibrated letters in the same pool (`letter-types.md`, *Israeli calls*).

**Why.** Restrained letters read as faint praise to American readers and effusive ones as
uncalibrated to British readers [trodden; dailynous; leiter-thread]; letter norms differ by
country of origin in CS master's applications [zhao2023]; AI suggestions push non-Western
writers toward Western styles [agarwal2025].

## 5. Sentences and words

Use `writing-guide.md`. The entries that matter most in letters:

- *Strong sentences let the subject perform the most important verb* — the candidate is the
  subject of the sentences that praise them.
- *You can control the stress, the emphasis* — end the rank sentence on the rank.
- *Short statements are strong* — the verdict sentence.
- *Use strengthening words sparingly to emphasize what you really care about* — one
  superlative, attached to the rank.
- *Every word should count, avoid fillers*.
- *Explain, don't acknowledge*.

## Mechanics

Fixed directly and listed, never presented as a finding that leads the queue. They cost the
writer nothing and change little for the reader.

### Template residue and names

**Defect.** Placeholder text from a template, a TODO, a wrong program or candidate name,
inconsistent pronouns or titles, a stale date.

**Cue.** `letter_check.py` flags template sentences and TODOs; check every name against the
call and the candidate macro.

**Repair.** Delete residue; fix names, macros and pronouns. Use the candidate macro
(`name`) or the literal name consistently, not both.

**Boundary.** A deleted template paragraph may have been holding a place for content: say
what was there.

**Why.** Wrong-program names and leftover template text mark a letter as reused [krishnamurthi;
cuny-samples].
