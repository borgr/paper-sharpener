# Letter Types

Read the section for the letter in hand. Each section gives the reader, the question the
letter must answer, the **expected items** (what the close reader will notice is missing,
used by *The close reader looks for what is missing* in `letter-guide.md`), length, and
pitfalls. Rules change every cycle: the call text overrides this file. Figures marked
*(check)* were not confirmed for the current cycle.

**A call with no section here** (OGS, NSERC, Vanier, a lab's own form): take the expected items of
the nearest genre below, then read the call's own criteria and referee questions and add them.
Say which section you borrowed from.

## PhD and MSc admission

**Reader.** A faculty committee, then prospective advisors in the subfield; in centralized
European programs (ELLIS, IMPRS) a cross-site panel first.

**Question.** Can this person do sustained, independent, original research, and how do they
compare with others the writer has known? For a thesis MSc, research aptitude as well as
preparation.

**Expected items.**
- Relationship: role, duration, closeness of contact.
- A research story: project in the writer's words, the candidate's role, outcome, setbacks.
- Publication context: venue, status (preprint, workshop, main track), author convention.
- A rank against a defined pool, consistent with any grid on the form.
- Course context where relevant: rank in a class of stated size.
- A short writer paragraph for calibration, especially from a less-known institution.

**Grids.** US portals pair the letter with a ratings grid (Top 1/5/10/25/50%). The grid and
the letter must agree: a "top 1%" box needs the letter to justify it.

**Length.** 1–2 pages. Oxford: 500–1,000 words is typical, not a cap.

**Pitfalls.** Transcript restatement; mail-merge errors; famous writers who barely know the
student; for Europe, unqualified praise; for the US, a reserved letter without an explicit rank.

**Israeli PhD admission (Weizmann Feinberg, Technion, TAU, HUJI).** Admission is often direct,
to a named supervisor, and the committee is the institute's graduate school, mostly outside the
candidate's field. Translate CS conventions. The institutes' referee forms change; ask for the
current one *(form contents not verified)*.

**When the writer is also the prospective advisor.** Common in Israeli direct admission. Say so
in the first lines; the committee needs to know the letter is also a commitment. Then add what
only an advisor-to-be can give: the planned research direction and why this candidate fits it,
the supervision arrangement (including co-advisors), and that funding and a place in the group
are in hand if that is the institute's rule. Keep the evaluation separate from the enthusiasm
of a recruiter: the rank and the evidence carry the same weight as in any other letter, and the
reader discounts praise from someone who wants the candidate.

## When the writer is not the supervisor

A co-mentor, collaborator or senior colleague writes a different letter from the advisor's.

- **Say the role in the first lines,** and how the work was shared with the supervisor. The
  reader will have the advisor's letter too, and needs to know which part this writer saw.
- **Credit the part you saw.** Name the project you did together and the candidate's share of
  it; leave the rest of the record to the advisor.
- **Rank against the pool you know.** A collaborator's pool is "the students and junior
  researchers I have worked with", not "my students". Size it all the same.
- **A cost to the writer is a strong signal only once the relationship is stated.** "I recommend
  him despite my own interest in keeping him" reads as calibration when the reader knows the
  writer works with him; before that, it reads as an employer letting someone go.

## Research engineer, staff and pre-doctoral positions

Read like a postdoc letter with the weight moved from independent research to delivery: what the
candidate built, who uses it, how fast they learn a new stack, and whether they would grow into a
researcher if that is the path. Rank against engineers or junior researchers the writer has
worked with.

## Industry PhD fellowships

**Reader.** Company researchers; Google and Apple first run a university nomination round.

**Question.** Is this an exceptional student whose research matters to the company's areas,
and likely to lead?

**Expected items.** The advisor's specific view of the student's contributions; impact
(adoption, open-source use, citations); independence and leadership; relevance to the
company's areas without a sales pitch.

| Program | Letters | Constraint |
|---|---|---|
| Google | 2–3, one from the advisor | Criteria: proposal, impact, achievement, leadership |
| Apple | 1–2, one from the advisor | 1 page per letter *(check)* |
| NVIDIA | 2–3, one from the advisor | Letters "carry substantial weight"; famous but distant referees do not help |
| Microsoft Research | 2 | Letterhead, signed, separate PDFs |
| Meta | 2 | Application stalls without both |

**Pitfall.** Confidential or proprietary research in the letter.

## Foundation and government fellowships

**NSF GRFP.** A multidisciplinary, non-specialist panel. The letter is about the person, not
the project, and must cover **both** Intellectual Merit and Broader Impacts, past and future.
Weak Broader Impacts sinks applicants.

**Hertz.** A form, not a letter. Answer each prompt (creativity, initiative, applied problem
solving, comparison with peers at the same stage) with an example.

**Rhodes.** Two pages, of which only the first two are read. Address the Selection Committee.
Expected: the writer and relationship, how the candidate will grow, what the file does not
show, the single most important thing. Academic referees give the band (top 1/3/5/10/25%),
the cohort size and the basis (personal assessment or official rank). Letters must not be
edited or curated by others before upload. Disclose conflicts.

**Marshall.** At most 1,000 words. Academic merit, leadership, "ambassadorial potential", fit
with the UK course. British readers distrust relentless praise; one considered qualification
helps.

**Fulbright.** Feasibility of this project in this host country, preparation, adaptability.
Answer the form's questions.

## Israeli calls

Formal requirements are strict everywhere: addressee, letterhead, signature, order, English.
Committees are multidisciplinary, so translate CS conventions (`letter-guide.md`, *Describe
the research, and translate its currency*). An understated Israeli letter competes against
American-calibrated ones in the same pool.

**Rothschild (postdoc abroad).** Two letters, one from the supervisor, plus an encouraged
letter from a senior academic at another university. Expected: comparison with (i) scholars
at a similar stage, (ii) the referee's own close students, (iii) where possible, past
Rothschild Fellows. A host who wrote an invitation letter may not also recommend.

**Azrieli International Postdoctoral Fellowship (incoming).** Two ordinary referee letters,
plus a separate **Academic Sponsor letter** (see [Sponsor and host letters](#sponsor-and-host-letters)).

**CHE / PBC Outstanding Women postdoc abroad** (and the parallel tracks). 2–3 letters, one
from the doctoral advisor; a separate 8–10 line Dean's statement; faculties rank their
nominees. Speak to future faculty potential in Israel, since that is the program's purpose.

**Zuckerman (postdoc to the US).** Three letters, preferably one from outside the home
university. Selection weighs leadership, so the letters should address it.

**Clore (PhD).** At least three signed letters, supervisors first, on institutional
letterhead, **addressed to the Clore Scholars Programme**.

## Postdoc positions

**Reader.** The hiring PI, an expert in the area.

**Question.** Will this person be productive and independent quickly, bring new skills, and
be on a path to faculty or research leadership?

**Expected items.** Thesis contributions in technical detail and the candidate's share of
multi-author work; independence (ideas originated, direction needed, setbacks handled);
transferable skills; mentoring of juniors; a comparison with your past students or postdocs.

**Pitfalls.** A group effort credited without saying what the candidate did; no word on
independence; a recycled PhD-admission letter.

## Faculty jobs

**Reader.** A search committee often outside the subfield, then the department and the dean.

**Question.** Is this candidate among the very best of the cohort, and will they build an
independent, high-impact program, attract students and funding, and teach?

**Expected items.**
- An explicit top-tier placement among the writer's students or postdocs, and the tier of
  department the candidate fits ("I would hire her here").
- A comparison with the cohort on the market at the same stage.
- The research vision and its independence from the advisor's agenda.
- One or two signature contributions with their impact and the candidate's role.
- Mentoring and teaching.

**Named peers.** CS letters often name peers ("comparable to X and Y when they were on the
market"). Do so only when you know them well and the comparison is fair and favorable;
otherwise compare against a defined pool.

**Length.** 2–3 pages.

**Pitfalls.** Lukewarm enthusiasm, which a famous name does not rescue; a missing advisor
letter (the candidate's problem, but worth telling them).

## Tenure, promotion and external review

The genre is evaluative, not advocacy. Every default about hedging and limitations changes:
state real limitations plainly, and say why they are outweighed if they are.

**Arm's length.** No advising, co-authorship, collaboration or shared funding (often within
five years), no close personal tie, not employed at the candidate's institution. Disclose any
relationship in the first lines; if there is a conflict, decline and give the reason.

**Reader.** Department, then college and university committees, the provost; mostly outside
the field. Some systems pass a redacted copy to the candidate.

**Question.** Does the record meet **the requesting institution's** stated standard for the
rank, judged by an independent expert?

**Expected items.**
- Your expertise and its relation to the candidate's work.
- An early summary verdict in the institution's own terms ("national impact", "distinguished").
- Evidence from specific works: thesis, significance, quality, venue, impact (citations,
  adoption, invited talks).
- Comparison with others at a similar career stage, naming leading departments in the area.
- Field norms explained for outsiders (conferences as archival venues; author order).
- An answer to every question in the charge letter, and to "would they be tenured at your
  institution?" only if asked; some institutions forbid it.
- A clear conclusion.

**Length.** 2–4 pages; a roadmap sentence helps in long letters.

**Pitfalls.** Vague or hedged language, read as negative; applying your own institution's bar
when asked not to; text left from another case.

## Sponsor and host letters

Azrieli sponsor letters and MSCA letters of commitment are **commitments**, not
recommendations. On the host's letterhead, cover the candidate's qualifications and fit,
their contribution to the project, facilities and environment, synergy with the team, a
mentorship plan, and acknowledgement of the host's obligations. An Azrieli sponsor may back
one candidate only. A Rothschild host invitation must not be a recommendation.

## Visa expert letters

O-1A, EB-1A and EB-1B letters are legal evidence read by a non-expert officer. Map specific
contributions to the regulatory criteria (above all "original contributions of major
significance"), state the writer's credentials and basis of knowledge, and corroborate with
citations, adoption and awards. Independent experts weigh more than collaborators; letters
that repeat the definitions or read as templates persuade little. Usually drafted with the
candidate's lawyer.

## Award endorsements

Personal testimony from the endorser's own knowledge, about the contribution the award
recognizes, at the level the award requires. Endorsers from different institutions;
endorsements with no specific content fail. Some awards (ACM) require an ethics attestation.

## Industry references

Verifiable facts, examples of job performance and growth areas, often by phone or a short
form. Avoid statements the writer cannot substantiate.
